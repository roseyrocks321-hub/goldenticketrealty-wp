5 Questions to Ask a Cash Home Buyer Before You Sign Anything

Published: December 30, 2026 | Golden Ticket Realty | Melbourne, FL

Most "we buy houses" companies are legitimate. Some aren't. And because cash sales move fast—days instead of months—sellers have less time to notice when something's off. The good news: five questions, asked before you sign anything, will filter out almost every bad actor in the Melbourne market. We buy houses for a living at Golden Ticket Realty, and we encourage every seller to ask us these exact questions. A buyer with nothing to hide will answer every one of them easily. Here's what to ask, what good answers sound like, and the red flags that should end the conversation.

Question 1: "Can you show me proof of funds?"

A cash buyer claiming to pay cash should be able to show that the cash exists. Legitimate buyers can provide a recent bank statement or a letter from their financial institution demonstrating sufficient funds to close. Many will redact account numbers—that's normal and fine. What matters is that the document comes from a real financial institution and covers the purchase price.

Why it matters: some "cash buyers" aren't buying at all—they're wholesalers who tie up your home under contract at a low price, then shop that contract to real investors for a markup. If they can't find someone, the deal collapses weeks after you thought it was done. Asking for proof of funds on day one filters most of them out instantly.

Good answer: documentation produced without hesitation, plus a straightforward explanation of where funds come from (personal capital, a business account, a credit line for flips).
Red flag: "We don't share financials," vague references to "investors," or a request for time to "line up funding" after you've signed.

Question 2: "Can you share local references and recent closings?"

Real estate is local, and reputation is trackable. Ask for the names of title companies or closing attorneys they've used recently in Brevard County, and the names of sellers (or at least neighborhoods and streets) they've bought from in the last year. Then actually verify: call the title company, search the buyer's name in Brevard County public records, or ask for a brief phone call with a past seller.

Why it matters: established local buyers close through the same title companies repeatedly, and those records are public. A buyer who has genuinely closed dozens of Melbourne-area homes will have a paper trail you can check in an afternoon.

Good answer: specific local references, recent Brevard County closings they're happy for you to verify, and a willingness to connect you with past sellers.
Red flag: out-of-state operations with no local closings, anonymous testimonials, or pushback when you ask to verify anything.

Question 3: "Who pays closing costs, and are there any fees?"

With a direct cash sale, the answer should be simple: the buyer pays the closing costs, and there are no fees charged to you—no "processing fee," no "assignment fee," no cancellation fee. The seller's proceeds at closing should equal the contract price minus anything you've agreed in writing to pay off (a mortgage payoff, liens, prorated property taxes, HOA dues—normal stuff that's itemized on the settlement statement).

Why it matters: this is where advance-fee scams live. In a classic version, a fake buyer "guarantees" a wonderful price and then demands an upfront fee—for "administrative processing," an "appraisal," a "deposit to hold the offer"—before closing. Real buyers never need your money to buy your house. Any fee a seller is asked to pay before closing deserves extreme scrutiny.

Good answer: "We pay all closing costs. You pay nothing, and you'll see every deduction itemized on the settlement statement at closing."
Red flag: any upfront fee, ever, for any reason.

Question 4: "Walk me through the contract—earnest money, inspection period, and exit clauses."

A fair cash purchase agreement is short and readable. Three provisions deserve your attention:

  • Earnest money. A serious buyer puts a deposit down, held by a neutral title company or attorney—never paid directly to the buyer. It should be a meaningful amount, credited to you if the buyer defaults. Earnest money is your compensation if they walk away.
  • Inspection period. Professional buyers do a quick inspection to confirm condition—that's legitimate. But the window should be short (days, not weeks) and the contract should say clearly what happens at the end of it. What you don't want is a long, open-ended inspection window that exists mainly to give the buyer leverage to renegotiate the price later.
  • Exit clauses. Read every contingency. A buyer whose offer depends on "partner approval," "feasibility," or unspecified "due diligence" hasn't really made you an offer—they've made you a placeholder while they shop your contract around.

Why it matters: the most common scam-adjacent tactic in this industry isn't illegality—it's renegotiation after signing. A buyer contracts at one price, then returns after the inspection window claiming newfound problems and demanding a lower price, betting you won't restart the process. Clear contract terms are your only protection.

Good answer: the buyer explains each clause in plain English, encourages you to have an attorney review it (we do—see our FAQs), and offers earnest money with a short, defined inspection window.
Red flag: pressure to sign immediately, contracts that are strangely long and full of contingencies, or any reluctance to let a third party hold the earnest money.

Question 5: "How did you arrive at this offer number?"

A professional buyer should be able to explain the offer—not just state it. The honest math usually looks like this: what comparable renovated homes in your Melbourne neighborhood sell for, minus the cost of the renovation your home needs, minus holding and selling costs, minus a reasonable margin for the risk and work involved. If a buyer can't or won't explain their number, they either don't understand it or don't want you to.

Why it matters: transparency here is the single best predictor of how the rest of the transaction will go. Buyers who explain their math tend to honor their contracts. Buyers who say "take it or leave it" tend to create surprises.

Good answer: a clear breakdown referencing actual comparable sales in Brevard County and the specific repairs your home needs. You can see how we approach this on our how we buy houses page.
Red flag: numbers with no explanation, offers made sight-unseen over the phone within minutes, or hostility when you ask.

The Red Flag Cheat Sheet

Beyond the five questions, these behaviors should end any conversation, fast:

  • Pressure and artificial deadlines. "This offer is only good until tomorrow" exists to stop you from doing the due diligence this article describes. Real cash buyers can wait a day or two.
  • Upfront fees of any kind. Repeat it until it's instinct: legitimate buyers never need your money to buy your house.
  • Requests to sign over your deed early. Ownership transfers only at a licensed title company's closing table, at the same moment you receive your money. Anyone asking for your deed "in advance" is running a scam.
  • No verifiable local presence. A PO box, a call center, and no Brevard County closings you can verify.
  • Refusal to close through a title company. Closing without title insurance and escrow protection leaves you exposed to liens and ownership disputes that follow the property.
