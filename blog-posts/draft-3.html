Who Owns the House During a Florida Divorce?

Florida is an equitable distribution state. That means marital property, including the family home in most cases, gets divided fairly, which does not always mean exactly 50/50. Courts weigh factors like each spouse's contribution, the length of the marriage, and each person's financial circumstances.

A few common situations:

  • Both names on the title and mortgage: Both spouses typically have an ownership interest and remain on the hook for the loan until it is paid off or refinanced.
  • Only one name on the deed: The home can still be marital property if it was bought during the marriage or paid for with marital funds.
  • One spouse owned it before the marriage: It may be separate property, but increases in value during the marriage can still be subject to division.

Until there is a settlement or court order, the house is usually a joint asset, and major decisions about it should involve both spouses. This is where neutral ground matters: whoever you sell to should treat both of you equally, not quietly favor one side's timeline.

What Are Your Options for the Marital Home?

Divorcing couples in Melbourne and Brevard County generally have three realistic options:

  • One spouse keeps it: Usually a buyout of the other's share plus a refinance to remove them from the mortgage. This works when one person genuinely wants the home and can qualify alone, which is harder than many expect on a single income.
  • Co-own for a while: Some couples keep the house jointly until kids finish a school year or the market improves. It sounds practical, but it keeps both parties financially entangled and both credit scores exposed during a tense period.
  • Sell the house: Split the proceeds according to the settlement, pay off the mortgage, and close the chapter. It is the cleanest option financially, which is why it is the most common.

When selling is the decision, the next question is how. A traditional listing adds months of showings, staging, repair negotiations, and buyer contingencies to an already difficult year. A cash sale compresses all of that into days or weeks.

Why Do Couples Choose a Fast Cash Sale?

After handling these situations around Brevard County, the reasons couples choose a cash sale come up again and again:

  • Carrying costs stop bleeding both budgets. While the house sits unsold, the mortgage, insurance, taxes, and utilities keep coming, often out of two households' finances instead of one. Every month on market is money neither spouse gets back.
  • It removes the biggest source of conflict. Price reductions, repair requests, agent disagreements: a traditional sale creates dozens of decisions two divorcing people have to make together. A single as-is cash offer dramatically shrinks that list.
  • The split is simple. At closing, the title company pays off the mortgage and sends each spouse their agreed share. There is no arguing about who paid for what repair, because nobody made repairs.
  • Timeline certainty helps the divorce itself. Attorneys and mediators can finalize a settlement faster when the biggest asset has a guaranteed closing date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell my house before the divorce is final in Florida?

Often, yes, though the proceeds are handled according to your settlement agreement or a court order. Many couples sell early so the carrying costs stop and funds can be divided as agreed. Your attorney should confirm how proceeds are handled in your case.

Does my spouse have to agree to the sale?

If the home is a joint marital asset, yes, both spouses generally need to sign the deed at closing. A single neutral cash offer works well here: both parties get the same terms to accept or decline, rather than competing perspectives from agents or buyers.

What if my spouse is still living in the house?

That is common and not a problem for a cash sale. We buy homes with occupants in place, and closing can be scheduled with a reasonable move-out window so the transition stays smooth for everyone.

What if we cannot agree on anything?

When spouses disagree on repairs, pricing, or timing, a traditional listing magnifies the conflict. A straightforward as-is cash offer narrows the decision to one number and one closing date. If even that fails, the court may order the sale; an as-is cash sale satisfies that order quickly.

How are the proceeds divided at closing?

The title company pays off the mortgage and any agreed liens, then disburses the rest according to your settlement agreement or court order, usually wiring each spouse's share separately. Nothing passes through one spouse's hands first, so everything stays transparent.
